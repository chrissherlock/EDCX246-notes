The Limits of a Critical-Sociological Framework in Teacher Education: A Methodological, Historiographical and Pedagogical Critique of Making Sense of Mass Education (4th ed.)

A Critical Examination of Its Methodological Tensions, Explanatory Scope, and Pedagogical Implications

Abstract

This essay does not attempt to evaluate critical sociology, poststructuralism, or postcolonial theory as intellectual traditions in their entirety. Rather, the object of critique is the particular synthesis of sociological, genealogical, poststructuralist, and postcolonial approaches represented in Tait et al.'s Making Sense of Mass Education (4th ed., 2023), considering whether the explanatory claims advanced within that synthesis are sufficient to support the broader methodological and pedagogical conclusions drawn from them. The central problem examined here is not whether social structures influence educational outcomes, but whether identifying those influences warrants treating structural explanations as sufficient accounts of those outcomes. While the text provides a valuable diagnostic apparatus for examining structural inequalities and institutional sorting mechanisms, difficulties arise when a diagnostic framework is allowed to become an exclusive explanatory framework. By analysing the text's evidentiary framing, historiographical treatment of childhood, extensions of disciplinary-power analysis, and accounts of social reproduction, this essay argues that insufficiently interrogated theoretical assumptions can leave developmental biology, assessment utility, and other important dimensions of teacher preparation under-theorised.

I. Introduction: The Diagnostic Utility and Methodological Boundaries of Critical Theory

Critical sociology of education offers valuable analytical resources for examining historical inequalities and institutional sorting mechanisms. Tait et al. (2023), as an initial teacher education text, provides a particular synthesis of these approaches. They identify ways in which schooling systems can reproduce social stratification while presenting educational outcomes as products of ostensibly neutral meritocratic processes, exposing how middle-class cultural capital and institutional familiarity can function as implicit criteria shaping access and educational success (Bourdieu, 1986, pp. 243–252). Furthermore, they highlight the performative pressures generated by corporate audit cultures and standardized testing regimes (Ball, 2003; Lingard, 2010).

Yet, acknowledging these diagnostic contributions does not exempt the underlying theoretical frameworks from rigorous intellectual scrutiny. The argument advanced here does not require rejecting the validity of structural, Foucauldian, or constructivist analysis. Indeed, the explanatory value of these approaches is often greatest precisely where they identify institutional mechanisms that are difficult to observe from an individualistic perspective. Nor does recognising biological, cognitive, linguistic, developmental, or individual factors entail a return to simplistic methodological individualism; these factors can be incorporated within a multi-level account of educational causation. The issue is one of explanatory scope: no single level of analysis should be assumed to exhaust a complex educational phenomenon. The argument therefore concerns the scope of explanation rather than the truth or falsity of individual theoretical propositions: a proposition may be valid within one level of analysis while remaining insufficient as a general account of educational phenomena.

Three distinct standards should therefore be separated. A framework may be diagnostically adequate if it identifies genuine structural mechanisms; it may be causally adequate only if those mechanisms sufficiently explain the phenomenon under investigation; and it may be pedagogically adequate only if it provides sufficient guidance for understanding and responding to that phenomenon in practice. The argument developed here is that the particular strengths examined in this essay are primarily diagnostic. Difficulties emerge when the explanatory implications of such a diagnostic framework are extended beyond the institutional mechanisms it directly identifies, particularly when it is taken to bear on comprehensive educator preparation without sufficient attention to developmental biology, individual agency, disciplinary knowledge, assessment utility, or competing empirical explanations. This does not require the volume itself to provide a comprehensive account of every educational phenomenon; rather, the question is whether the explanatory emphases of an initial teacher-education text provide sufficient conceptual resources for the professional decisions to which those emphases are expected to contribute. The relevant question is therefore not whether these frameworks generate useful insights, but whether their explanatory claims remain proportionate to the evidence used to support them.

II. Methodological Tensions and the Framing of "Myths"

A central methodological tension within the volume involves its approach to empirical evidence. Tait et al. (2023) frequently employ the organizational device of "myths" to examine conventional educational assumptions, adopting a demythologising pedagogical posture. However, a qualitative sociology textbook does not become methodologically inconsistent merely because it relies upon discourse analysis rather than controlled laboratory experiments. The methodological question is not whether qualitative, historical, or philosophical reasoning is legitimate, but whether comparable empirical and conceptual claims are subjected to appropriately comparable standards of justification.

This potential asymmetry becomes particularly important where historical interpretations are presented as explanatory foundations for broader claims about education and development. For instance, the discussion of pre-adulthood draws upon Philippe Ariès's (1962) influential thesis concerning the historical construction of childhood—a thesis that has subsequently been substantially qualified by medieval historians. The problem is not Tait et al.'s use of Ariès itself, nor the legitimacy of historical interpretation, but the evidentiary status accorded to the resulting claim. As Orme (2001, pp. 4–5, 9) documents, medieval societies recognised childhood as a differentiated stage of life, even though the social meaning, legal status, expectations, and institutional treatment of children differed substantially from modern conceptions. The historical evidence therefore complicates any strong formulation in which childhood is presented primarily as a modern institutional invention. Furthermore, when the development of mass schooling is interpreted primarily through its relationship with industrialisation and social regulation, such an account requires careful differentiation between the economic functions of schooling, the administrative requirements generated by mass education, and stronger claims regarding institutional design. The methodological question is therefore whether historical and qualitative interpretations are subjected to sufficiently explicit evidentiary qualification when they are used to support broader claims about educational development.

III. Analogical Extension, Disciplinary Power, and the Psy-Complex

The application of Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power to everyday school administration represents another area where sociological theory risks overextending its explanatory reach. Foucault's analysis provides a useful vocabulary for examining how timetables, record-keeping, surveillance, and examination can contribute to the regulation of conduct. The methodological difficulty arises when the identification of disciplinary mechanisms is allowed to substitute for an explanation of the institutional purposes those mechanisms serve. School attendance, spatial organisation, and supervision may indeed produce forms of visibility and normalisation; however, their educational, administrative, and child-protection functions cannot be inferred from their disciplinary characteristics alone.

Mass education necessarily involves substantial spatial, temporal, and administrative coordination, and these functions cannot be adequately understood solely in terms of surveillance. An analysis that foregrounds surveillance without adequately accounting for these functions risks presenting legitimate forms of professional coordination primarily as mechanisms of social control. Similarly, in its discussion of subjectivity and the psy-complex, the text places substantial emphasis on the institutional classification and governance of conditions such as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 134–136; Rose, 1999)[cite: 3, 4, 5]. A sociological account of how ADHD is diagnosed, classified, and institutionally governed is analytically distinct from an account of the aetiology of ADHD itself[cite: 3, 4, 5]. The former can illuminate the institutional production and use of diagnostic categories without displacing the latter[cite: 3, 4, 5]. A comprehensive account also requires engagement with evidence concerning neurodevelopment, cognition, genetics, and environmental interaction (Demontis et al., 2023; Faraone et al., 2015; Thapar et al., 2017). Without such engagement, a predominantly socio-political account risks leaving insufficient analytical space for established neurodevelopmental evidence.

IV. Meritocracy, Datafication, and Explanatory Overreach

Underpinning these theoretical positions are recurring argumentative problems regarding institutional incentives, measurement, and individual variation. In examining social class and equity (Chapter 1), the text critiques meritocracy by foregrounding the ways systemic background conditions shape educational outcomes (Tait et al., 2023, pp. XX–XX). However, a structural critique becomes theoretically vulnerable if structural conditioning is treated as substantially determining educational outcomes while leaving insufficient analytical space for individual effort, prior knowledge, motivation, and cognitive development. Recognition of structural inequality is not incompatible with recognising individual agency, prior knowledge, motivation, and cognitive development as additional explanatory factors.

Similarly, when addressing standardized testing and datafication (Chapter 9), the treatment of standardised assessment places substantial emphasis on its relationship to datafication, governance, and surveillance. A treatment that places insufficient emphasis on the informational and evaluative functions of standardised assessment risks underweighting purposes that such assessments can serve, including monitoring attainment, identifying disparities, evaluating programmes, and informing system-level accountability. Furthermore, the framework can exhibit limitations of explanatory scope. A macro-level analysis of neoliberal policy (Chapter 7) can leave insufficient analytical space for variation in how teachers interpret, negotiate, adapt, and resist policy requirements in local practice. A related limitation arises in the treatment of pre-adulthood (Chapter 8), where an emphasis on social construction may understate the explanatory significance of biological maturation and developmental processes.

V. Bourdieusian Theory and the Risk of Deterministic Interpretation

Pierre Bourdieu’s (1986) framework of cultural capital, habitus, and symbolic violence provides useful analytical tools for understanding the reproduction of social privilege. However, difficulties arise when interpretations treat habitus as overwhelmingly determinative rather than as a structured disposition that interacts with shifting fields. Treating habitus as sufficiently determinative of individual conduct risks converting Bourdieu's theory of structured dispositions into a deterministic model of behaviour, minimizing human agency, variation in individual trajectories, and the capacity of explicit instruction to improve domain learning across diverse socioeconomic backgrounds.

This theoretical tension has potential implications for classroom practice. When academic English and formal disciplinary knowledge are interpreted predominantly as mechanisms through which existing power relations are reproduced, well-meaning educators may receive insufficient guidance concerning the pedagogical importance of explicitly teaching the linguistic and disciplinary conventions required for participation in academic and professional institutions. As Lisa Delpit (1995) argued in her pedagogical critiques, recognizing the historical contingency of linguistic authority does not entail withholding access to the forms of language that carry institutional power. Where prior exposure to dominant linguistic and disciplinary registers is unequally distributed across students' social environments, schools have the potential to provide explicit access to those registers. Failure to provide explicit access to such knowledge can therefore leave existing inequalities in access to institutional forms of knowledge insufficiently addressed.

A related issue arises in Basil Bernstein's (1971) sociolinguistic code theory. Bernstein's distinction between restricted and elaborated codes provides one historical sociological account of how linguistic practices may relate to social environments. This should not, however, be interpreted as implying that non-standard varieties are linguistically deficient. As William Labov (1972) demonstrated through his empirical work on Black English Vernacular, non-standard dialects possess complex internal grammatical structures. A sociological critique can therefore recognise academic language not solely as an instrument of class reproduction, but also as a public, learnable register that provides access to the forms of academic, professional, and institutional communication through which knowledge is conventionally taught, assessed, and exchanged.

VI. Poststructuralist Tensions in Gender, Identity, and Knowledge

The analytical tensions of critical sociology become particularly apparent in its treatment of gender, subjectivity, and sexuality through poststructuralist frameworks (Foucault, 1977; Butler, 1990). Within governmentality and performativity frameworks, agency is not necessarily denied but is conceptualised as operating within processes of subject formation in which persons are strongly constituted by social and discursive relations. If discursive constitution is treated as a sufficiently comprehensive explanation of action, however, the framework risks under-specifying agency and intentionality.

Moreover, the text navigates an analytical tension regarding identity categories. It adopts a deconstructive stance toward identity, analysing categories such as woman, man, and lesbian as historically contingent and regulatory categories constituted through relations of power. Yet, when addressing discrimination or systemic inequality, the framework subsequently relies upon these categories as relatively stable political and administrative classifications. Rather than an absolute logical contradiction, this represents an operational tension: using a category politically does not require believing that category possesses an immutable metaphysical essence. The difficulty therefore arises not from the political use of identity categories itself, but from failing to distinguish between their practical stability for purposes of policy and their theoretical contingency as objects of analysis. A predominantly discursive analysis of gender and sexuality may also leave material inequalities, including pay disparities and workplace discrimination, insufficiently theorised unless it is complemented by analysis of their economic and institutional dimensions.

Finally, postcolonial and constructivist critiques (Fanon, 1967; Spivak, 1988) must distinguish historical genealogy from the genetic fallacy: the historical or social origin of a proposition does not by itself determine whether that proposition is true or false. Acknowledging the colonial history of an academic discipline does not, by itself, establish that the knowledge produced within that discipline is epistemically invalid. This criticism should not be taken to imply that the volume rejects disciplinary knowledge or rational inquiry altogether; its treatment of philosophy and truth indicates that its position is more concerned with interrogating the conditions under which knowledge is produced and authorised than with simply denying the validity of disciplinary knowledge. The pedagogical difficulty arises when historical critique is allowed to displace rather than contextualise disciplinary knowledge. As Michael Young (2008) argues through Social Realism, specialised disciplinary knowledge should not be reduced to an expression of social power alone; access to such knowledge can provide learners with conceptual resources that extend beyond their immediate experiences.

VII. Pedagogical Implications of an Incomplete Explanatory Framework

The pedagogical concern is not that a sociology text must independently establish the mechanisms of cognition, development, or assessment, but that an initial teacher-education framework should not implicitly narrow the conceptual resources through which pre-service teachers are expected to understand those phenomena. The issue is therefore one of pedagogical completeness rather than a claim that critical sociology directly causes particular classroom behaviours. Most notably, if explicit instruction and institutionally valued academic codes are presented predominantly through the lens of symbolic violence, there is a potential pedagogical risk that pre-service teachers may become uncertain about the legitimacy of explicitly teaching the linguistic and disciplinary resources required for participation in academic and professional settings. Similarly, when organisational routines and disciplinary practices are interpreted predominantly through a framework of surveillance and control, pre-service teachers may receive an incomplete account of the legitimate organisational, instructional, and safeguarding purposes those practices can serve.

Furthermore, if a social-constructionist analysis of neurodevelopmental conditions is treated as explanatorily sufficient, it risks providing insufficient conceptual resources for recognising executive-function difficulties and considering appropriate accommodations. Conversely, a treatment of standardised assessment and quantitative metrics that is predominantly critical can understate their legitimate informational and administrative functions. These explanations need not be mutually exclusive: a sociological account may explain why a diagnostic category acquires particular institutional significance, while developmental science addresses the mechanisms underlying the condition being diagnosed. The methodological problem arises only when either level is treated as sufficient to explain the whole phenomenon. Finally, a deterministic interpretation of structural disadvantage could create a pedagogical risk: if educational outcomes are presented as largely predetermined by social position, teachers may have less conceptual space within the framework for understanding instruction as an intervention capable of changing those outcomes.

VIII. Conclusion: Critical Theory Within a Plural Explanatory Framework

The critical sociology of education provides a valuable diagnostic lens for identifying systemic inequalities, but it is insufficient as a standalone framework for comprehensive pedagogical practice. Its insights regarding cultural capital, audit cultures, and structural bias remain valuable for educator reflexivity (Ball, 2003; Lingard, 2010). However, difficulties arise when critical theory is extended beyond the institutional mechanisms it identifies, leaving insufficient analytical space for developmental biology, cognitive science, and the informational functions of assessment. Professional educator preparation requires a plural explanatory framework: structural analysis of inequality alongside evidence concerning development, cognition, language, assessment, subject knowledge, and pedagogy. The distinction between diagnostic, causal, and pedagogical adequacy is central to this argument: a framework may accurately identify a structural mechanism without establishing that the identified mechanism is causally sufficient, and an account that is causally informative may still fall short of providing the conceptual resources required for professional practice. These explanations need not be mutually exclusive: a sociological account may explain why a diagnostic category acquires particular institutional significance, while developmental science addresses the mechanisms underlying the condition being diagnosed. The point is not to replace sociological reductionism with biological or psychological reductionism, but to recognise that educational phenomena operate across multiple levels of explanation and that teacher preparation must equip educators to reason across those levels. The particular synthesis examined here is therefore most defensible when treated as a diagnostic resource within a broader framework of educational explanation, rather than as a comprehensive theory of educational causation or a complete basis for teacher preparation.
