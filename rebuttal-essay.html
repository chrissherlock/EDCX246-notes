From Diagnosis to Explanation: Methodological, Historiographical and Pedagogical Boundaries in Making Sense of Mass Education (4th ed.)

Abstract

This essay does not attempt to evaluate critical sociology, poststructuralism, or postcolonial theory as intellectual traditions in their entirety. Rather, the object of critique is the particular synthesis of sociological, genealogical, poststructuralist, and postcolonial approaches represented in Tait et al.'s Making Sense of Mass Education (4th ed., 2023), considering whether the explanatory claims advanced within that synthesis are sufficient to support the broader methodological and pedagogical conclusions drawn from them. The central problem examined here is not whether social structures influence educational outcomes, but whether identifying those influences warrants treating structural explanations as sufficient accounts of those outcomes. While the text provides a diagnostic apparatus for examining structural inequalities and institutional sorting mechanisms, difficulties arise when the explanatory implications of a diagnostic framework are extended beyond the mechanisms it directly identifies. By analysing the text's evidentiary framing, historiographical treatment of childhood, extensions of disciplinary-power analysis, and accounts of social reproduction, this essay argues that its explanatory emphasis can leave developmental biology, the informational functions of assessment, and other dimensions of teacher preparation insufficiently integrated.

I. Introduction: The Diagnostic Utility and Methodological Boundaries of Critical Theory

Critical sociology of education offers valuable analytical resources for examining historical inequalities and institutional sorting mechanisms. Tait et al. (2023), as an initial teacher education text, provides a particular synthesis of these approaches. They identify ways in which schooling systems can reproduce social stratification while presenting educational outcomes as products of ostensibly neutral meritocratic processes. Drawing on the concept of cultural capital, this analysis highlights how familiarity with institutionally valued practices can shape educational participation and success (Bourdieu, 1986, pp. 243–252). Furthermore, they highlight the performative pressures associated with contemporary systems of educational accountability and governance (Ball, 2003; Lingard, 2010).

Yet, acknowledging these diagnostic contributions does not exempt the underlying theoretical frameworks from rigorous intellectual scrutiny. The argument advanced here does not require rejecting the validity of structural, genealogical, or poststructuralist analysis. Indeed, the explanatory value of these approaches is often greatest precisely where they identify institutional mechanisms that are difficult to observe from an individualistic perspective. Nor does recognising biological, cognitive, linguistic, developmental, or individual factors entail a return to simplistic methodological individualism; these factors can be incorporated within a multi-level account of educational explanation. The central methodological problem is therefore not that the book's individual theoretical propositions are necessarily false, but that propositions that are illuminating at the institutional and socio-cultural levels can acquire excessive explanatory authority when extended to phenomena operating across several levels. A proposition may be valid within one level of analysis while remaining insufficient as a general account of educational phenomena. Showing that something is socially constructed, institutionally governed, historically contingent, or structurally conditioned does not by itself explain its developmental, cognitive, material, causal, or pedagogical dimensions. The argument therefore distinguishes between contradiction, omission, and explanatory insufficiency: the absence of a developmental, cognitive, material, or informational account does not make a sociological account false, but it limits the explanatory conclusions that can legitimately be drawn from it.

Three distinct standards should therefore be separated. A framework may be diagnostically adequate for a particular phenomenon if it identifies genuine structural mechanisms relevant to that phenomenon; it may be explanatorily adequate if the mechanisms it identifies provide sufficient explanatory coverage of the phenomenon; and it may be pedagogically adequate only if it provides sufficient conceptual resources for understanding and responding to the phenomenon in practice. These forms of adequacy should not be conflated. Demonstrating that a phenomenon is historically contingent, institutionally governed, or socially stratified does not by itself establish the mechanisms through which it occurs, nor does it determine the appropriate pedagogical response. The argument developed here is that the particular strengths examined in this essay are primarily diagnostic. Difficulties emerge when the explanatory implications of such a diagnostic framework are extended beyond the mechanisms it directly identifies, particularly when its conceptual emphases are treated as sufficient for the purposes of educator preparation without sufficient attention to developmental biology, individual agency, disciplinary knowledge, assessment theory, and other empirically grounded explanatory levels. This does not require the volume itself to provide a comprehensive account of every educational phenomenon. The criticism is therefore not one of comprehensiveness in the encyclopedic sense, but of explanatory sufficiency where the text's concepts are used to interpret phenomena that operate across multiple levels and are subsequently expected to inform professional decision-making. The relevant question is therefore not whether these frameworks generate useful insights, but whether their explanatory claims remain proportionate to the evidence used to support them. A framework may successfully diagnose a phenomenon without thereby providing an adequate account of it, and an explanatory account does not automatically provide the conceptual resources required for pedagogical judgement.

II. Methodological Tensions and the Framing of "Myths"

A methodological tension within the volume concerns the evidentiary status accorded to some of its historical claims, particularly where contested historiographical interpretations are used to support broader claims about education and development. Tait et al. (2023) frequently employ the organisational device of "myths" to examine conventional educational assumptions, thereby adopting a demythologising mode of presentation. However, a qualitative sociology textbook does not become methodologically inconsistent merely because it relies upon discourse analysis rather than controlled laboratory experiments. The methodological question is whether the evidentiary standards applied are proportionate to the strength of the claims being made.

This potential asymmetry becomes particularly important where historical interpretations are presented as explanatory foundations for broader claims about education and development. For instance, the discussion of pre-adulthood draws upon Philippe Ariès's (1962) influential thesis concerning the historical construction of childhood—a thesis that has subsequently been substantially qualified by medieval historians. The problem is not Tait et al.'s use of Ariès itself, nor the legitimacy of historical interpretation, but the evidentiary status accorded to the resulting claim. As Orme (2001, pp. 4–5, 9) documents, medieval sources distinguish children as a recognisable stage of life, even though the social meaning, legal status, expectations, and institutional treatment of children differed substantially from modern conceptions. Evidence that medieval societies recognised childhood does not refute the latter claim; it instead places limits on stronger formulations that might imply childhood itself emerged as a modern invention. Furthermore, where the development of mass schooling is interpreted through its relationship with industrialisation and social regulation, the analysis needs to distinguish between the economic functions of schooling, the administrative requirements generated by mass education, and stronger claims about the historical purposes or design of educational institutions. The methodological question is therefore whether historical and qualitative interpretations are subjected to sufficiently explicit evidentiary qualification when they are used to support broader claims about educational development.

III. Analogical Extension, Disciplinary Power, and the Psy-Complex

The application of Foucault's (1977) analysis of disciplinary power to everyday school administration represents another area where sociological theory risks overextending its explanatory reach. Foucault's analysis provides a useful vocabulary for examining how timetables, record-keeping, surveillance, and examination can contribute to the regulation of conduct. The methodological difficulty arises when the identification of disciplinary mechanisms is treated as sufficient to explain the institutional functions, educational effects, or practical justification of those mechanisms. School attendance, spatial organisation, and supervision may indeed produce forms of visibility and normalisation; however, their educational, administrative, and safeguarding functions cannot be inferred from their disciplinary characteristics alone.

Mass education necessarily involves substantial spatial, temporal, and administrative coordination; an account of school organisation therefore cannot adequately explain these practices solely in terms of surveillance. An analysis that foregrounds surveillance without adequately accounting for these functions risks obscuring the educational, administrative, and safeguarding purposes such practices may serve alongside their disciplinary effects. Similarly, in its discussion of subjectivity and the psy-complex, the text places substantial emphasis on the institutional classification and governance of conditions such as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 134–136; Rose, 1999). A sociological account of how ADHD is diagnosed, classified, and institutionally governed is analytically distinct from an account of the aetiology of ADHD itself; the social construction and institutional governance of a diagnostic category are analytically distinct from the biological, developmental, cognitive, and environmental processes underlying the condition to which that category refers. The former can illuminate the institutional production and use of diagnostic categories without itself explaining the latter. The methodological limitation arises only if the sociological account is taken to provide an explanation of the condition itself; in that case, it would require supplementation by evidence concerning genetic architecture, neurodevelopment, cognition, and environmental influences (Demontis et al., 2023; Faraone et al., 2015; Thapar et al., 2017). If the sociological account were taken to explain the condition itself, the absence of engagement with relevant biological, developmental, cognitive, and environmental evidence would leave such an account incomplete.

IV. Meritocracy, Datafication, and Explanatory Overreach

The same methodological issue recurs in the treatment of meritocracy, assessment, policy, and developmental difference. In examining social class and equity (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 19–25), the text critiques meritocracy by foregrounding the ways systemic background conditions shape educational outcomes. However, such a structural critique becomes theoretically vulnerable if the explanatory weight assigned to structural conditioning leaves insufficient analytical space for variation in prior knowledge, motivation, cognition, learning histories, and individual responses to educational contexts. The relevant methodological question is therefore not whether structural conditions influence educational outcomes, but whether an account that foregrounds those conditions provides sufficient conceptual resources for explaining variation in outcomes among students who occupy different positions within the same structural environment.

Similarly, when addressing standardized testing and datafication (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 281–311), the discussion of standardised assessment places substantial emphasis on its relationship to datafication, governance, and surveillance. An account that places insufficient emphasis on the informational and evaluative functions of standardised assessment risks underweighting purposes for which such assessments may be used, including monitoring attainment, identifying disparities, contributing to programme evaluation, and informing system-level accountability. The relevant question is therefore not whether standardised assessments can have informational and evaluative functions, but whether those functions receive sufficient analytical attention alongside their governance and political uses.

Furthermore, the framework can exhibit limitations of explanatory scope. Where an analysis of neoliberal policy operates primarily at the macro-institutional level (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 141–164), it can leave insufficient analytical space for variation in how teachers interpret, negotiate, adapt, and resist policy requirements in practice. A related limitation arises in the treatment of pre-adulthood (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 165–181), where a social-constructionist analysis of pre-adulthood can illuminate the historical and institutional production of childhood categories without, by itself, explaining biological maturation or developmental processes.

V. Bourdieusian Theory and the Risk of Deterministic Interpretation

Pierre Bourdieu's (1986) account of cultural capital provides a useful analytical tool for understanding the reproduction of social privilege. The methodological difficulty arises when Bourdieusian concepts are interpreted deterministically. In particular, treating socially structured dispositions as strongly predictive of individual conduct can leave inadequate conceptual space for agency, individual variation, changing circumstances, and pedagogical intervention. The pedagogical risk of such a deterministic interpretation—understating the capacity of instruction to alter students' access to valued forms of knowledge and practice—therefore depends on the extent to which the framework leaves sufficient conceptual space for agency and pedagogical intervention.

This theoretical tension has potential implications for classroom practice. When institutionally valued linguistic practices and formal disciplinary knowledge are interpreted predominantly as mechanisms through which existing power relations are reproduced, the framework may provide less explicit guidance concerning the pedagogical importance of explicitly teaching the linguistic and disciplinary conventions required for participation in academic and professional institutions. Delpit (1995), in arguing for explicit access to the codes of power, provides a pedagogical basis for distinguishing recognition of linguistic inequality from withholding access to institutionally powerful forms of language. Where prior exposure to institutionally valued linguistic and disciplinary registers is unequally distributed across students' social environments, schools can therefore provide an important site for explicitly teaching access to those registers. This supports an argument for providing students with explicit access to institutionally consequential linguistic and disciplinary conventions, without treating students' home varieties as linguistically deficient.

A related issue arises in Basil Bernstein's (1971) sociolinguistic code theory. Bernstein's distinction between restricted and elaborated codes provides one historical sociological account of how linguistic practices may relate to social environments and patterns of communication. This should not, however, be interpreted as implying that non-standard varieties are linguistically deficient. As William Labov (1972) demonstrated through his empirical work on Black English Vernacular, non-standard dialects possess complex internal grammatical structures. Labov's work therefore provides empirical grounds for rejecting the characterisation of non-standard dialects as linguistically deficient. Neither account, however, by itself resolves the separate pedagogical question of whether students should also receive explicit access to institutionally consequential academic and disciplinary registers. Delpit (1995) provides a basis for addressing that question without treating students' home varieties as linguistically deficient: academic language can be understood as a learnable institutional register that provides access to the forms of academic, professional, and institutional communication through which knowledge is conventionally taught, assessed, and exchanged.

VI. Poststructuralist Tensions in Gender, Identity, and Knowledge

The analytical tensions of critical sociology become particularly apparent in its treatment of gender, subjectivity, and sexuality through poststructuralist and performativity frameworks (Butler, 1990; Tait et al., 2023). Within these frameworks, agency is not necessarily denied but is conceptualised as operating within processes of subject formation in which persons are constituted through social and discursive relations. If discursive constitution is treated as a sufficiently comprehensive explanation of action, however, the framework risks under-specifying agency and intentionality.

Moreover, the text navigates an analytical tension regarding identity categories (Tait et al., 2023, pp. 110–135). The discussion approaches identity categories through a deconstructive lens, treating categories such as woman, man, and lesbian as historically contingent and examining their relationship to power and regulation. Yet analyses of discrimination and systemic inequality often rely upon these categories as practically operative political and administrative classifications. Historical contingency does not make a category unusable; political and administrative categories can remain operationally significant precisely because institutions act upon them. The analytical difficulty arises where the practical stability of such categories for purposes of policy is not clearly distinguished from their theoretical contingency as objects of analysis. Where the analysis of gender and sexuality remains predominantly discursive, material and institutional dimensions of inequality may be insufficiently theorised unless complemented by analysis of their economic and organisational conditions.

Finally, the treatment of knowledge and truth in the volume raises a methodological distinction between the historical conditions under which knowledge acquires authority and the separate question of its truth or epistemic status. A genealogical explanation of the historical and institutional conditions under which knowledge is produced and authorised does not, by itself, determine the epistemic status of that knowledge. This criticism should not be taken to imply that the volume rejects disciplinary knowledge or rational inquiry altogether; its treatment of philosophy and truth can be read as placing greater emphasis on interrogating the conditions under which knowledge is produced and authorised than on denying the validity of disciplinary knowledge. The pedagogical difficulty arises when historical critique is allowed to displace rather than contextualise disciplinary knowledge. As Michael Young (2008) argues through social realism, the social conditions under which specialised disciplinary knowledge is produced and authorised should be distinguished from questions concerning its epistemic status. This distinction parallels the broader methodological argument of this essay: explaining how knowledge is socially produced and authorised does not by itself establish its epistemic status, just as establishing epistemic status does not by itself determine how that knowledge should be taught or used pedagogically, and access to such knowledge can provide learners with conceptual resources that extend beyond their immediate experiences.

VII. Pedagogical Implications of an Incomplete Explanatory Framework

This distinction becomes particularly important because the volume is not presented merely as a specialist contribution to sociological theory but as a textbook for students of education and teacher preparation. The relevant evaluative standard is therefore not whether its sociological analyses exhaust the phenomena under discussion, but whether the conceptual resources it foregrounds are sufficiently distinguished from the additional forms of knowledge required for professional judgement. The pedagogical concern is not that a sociology text must independently establish the mechanisms of cognition, development, or assessment, but whether an initial teacher-education framework might implicitly narrow the conceptual resources through which pre-service teachers encounter those phenomena. The implications identified here are therefore best understood as theoretical pedagogical risks arising from the framework's explanatory emphases, rather than empirically demonstrated effects on pre-service teachers. Most notably, if explicit instruction and institutionally valued academic codes are presented predominantly through the lens of symbolic violence, there is a potential pedagogical risk that the framework may give pre-service teachers less conceptual space for understanding the pedagogical rationale for explicitly teaching the linguistic and disciplinary resources required for participation in academic and professional settings. Similarly, when organisational routines and disciplinary practices are interpreted predominantly through a framework of surveillance and control, the framework may provide an incomplete account of the organisational, instructional, and safeguarding purposes those practices can serve.

Furthermore, if an analysis of the social construction and institutional governance of neurodevelopmental diagnoses were treated as explanatorily sufficient, it could provide insufficient conceptual resources for understanding attentional and executive-function difficulties that may be learning-relevant and for considering appropriate supports. Conversely, a treatment of standardised assessment and quantitative metrics that is predominantly critical can understate their informational and administrative functions. A sociological account may explain why a diagnostic category acquires particular institutional significance, while developmental science addresses mechanisms underlying the condition being diagnosed. The methodological problem arises only when either level is treated as sufficient to explain the whole phenomenon. Finally, an overly deterministic interpretation of structural disadvantage could create a pedagogical risk: if educational outcomes are treated as strongly constrained by social position without sufficient attention to instructional effects and individual variation, the framework may provide less conceptual space for understanding instruction as an intervention capable of changing those outcomes.

VIII. Conclusion: Critical Theory Within a Plural Explanatory Framework

The synthesis of critical, genealogical, poststructuralist, and postcolonial approaches represented in Making Sense of Mass Education provides a valuable diagnostic lens for identifying systemic inequalities, but its explanatory resources are insufficient on their own when addressing the broader range of explanatory and pedagogical questions encountered in teacher preparation. Its insights regarding cultural capital, audit cultures, and structural bias remain valuable for educator reflexivity (Ball, 2003; Lingard, 2010). However, difficulties arise when the synthesis's explanatory concepts are extended beyond the institutional mechanisms it identifies, leaving insufficient analytical space for developmental, biological, and cognitive evidence, as well as the informational functions of assessment. The analysis therefore points toward a plural explanatory framework for professional educator preparation: structural analysis of inequality alongside evidence concerning development, cognition, language, assessment, subject knowledge, and pedagogy. The distinction between diagnostic, explanatory, and pedagogical adequacy is central to this argument. A framework may accurately identify a structural mechanism without establishing that the identified mechanism adequately explains the phenomenon as a whole, and an account may be explanatorily informative while still falling short of providing the conceptual resources required for professional practice. The resulting framework is therefore additive rather than substitutive: structural explanation should be retained while being situated alongside developmental, cognitive, linguistic, disciplinary, assessment, and pedagogical explanations. The point is not to replace sociological reductionism with biological or psychological reductionism, but to recognise that educational phenomena operate across multiple levels of explanation and that teacher preparation consequently benefits from equipping educators to reason across those levels. The particular synthesis examined here is therefore most defensible when treated as a diagnostic resource within a broader framework of educational explanation, rather than as a sufficient framework for explaining educational phenomena across the multiple levels relevant to teacher preparation.
