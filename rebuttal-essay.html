The Limits of a Critical-Sociological Framework in Teacher Education: A Methodological, Historiographical and Pedagogical Critique of Making Sense of Mass Education (4th ed.)

A Critical Examination of Its Methodological Tensions, Structural Overreach, and Pedagogical Implications

Abstract

This essay does not attempt to evaluate critical sociology, poststructuralism, or postcolonial theory as intellectual traditions in their entirety. Rather, the object of critique is the particular synthesis of sociological, genealogical, poststructuralist, and postcolonial approaches represented in Tait et al.'s Making Sense of Mass Education (4th ed., 2023), considering whether those claims can bear the methodological and pedagogical implications sometimes attached to them. The central problem examined here is not whether social structures influence educational outcomes, but whether identifying those influences warrants treating structural explanations as sufficient accounts of those outcomes. While the text provides a valuable diagnostic apparatus for examining structural inequalities and institutional sorting mechanisms, difficulties arise when a diagnostic framework is allowed to become an exclusive explanatory framework. By analyzing the text's selective evidentiary treatment, historiographical framing regarding childhood, analogical extensions of carceral surveillance, and deterministic readings of social reproduction, this essay argues that insufficiently interrogated theoretical assumptions can discount developmental biology, obscure assessment utility, and consequently leave important dimensions of teacher preparation under-theorised.

I. Introduction: The Diagnostic Utility and Methodological Boundaries of Critical Theory

It is analytically productive to engage with the critical sociology of education, as initial teacher education texts such as Tait et al. (2023) offer valuable insights into historical inequalities and institutional sorting mechanisms. They successfully identify how schooling systems frequently reproduce social stratification under the guise of neutral meritocracy, exposing how middle-class cultural capital and institutional familiarity operate as unacknowledged entry criteria (Bourdieu, 1986, pp. 243–252). Furthermore, they highlight the performative pressures generated by corporate audit cultures and standardized testing regimes (Ball, 2003; Lingard, 2010).

Yet, acknowledging these diagnostic contributions does not exempt the underlying theoretical frameworks from rigorous intellectual scrutiny. The argument advanced here does not require rejecting the validity of structural, Foucauldian, or constructivist analysis. Indeed, the explanatory value of these approaches is often greatest precisely where they identify institutional mechanisms that are difficult to observe from an individualistic perspective. Nor does recognition of biological, cognitive, or individual factors entail a return to simplistic methodological individualism; these factors can be incorporated within a multi-level account of educational causation. The issue is one of explanatory scope: no single level of analysis should be assumed to exhaust a complex educational phenomenon.

Three distinct standards should therefore be separated. A framework may be diagnostically adequate if it identifies genuine structural mechanisms; it may be causally adequate only if those mechanisms sufficiently explain the phenomenon under investigation; and it may be pedagogically adequate only if it provides sufficient guidance for understanding and responding to that phenomenon in practice. The argument developed here is that the strengths of Making Sense of Mass Education are primarily diagnostic. Difficulties emerge when a diagnostic framework transitions from an auxiliary analytical lens into an exclusive explanatory framework for educator preparation, particularly where it discounts developmental biology, individual agency, disciplinary knowledge, assessment utility, or competing empirical explanations. The relevant question is therefore not whether these frameworks generate useful insights, but whether their explanatory claims remain proportionate to the evidence used to support them.

II. Methodological Asymmetry and the Pedagogical Framing of "Myths"

A central methodological tension within the volume involves its approach to empirical evidence. Tait et al. (2023) frequently employ the organizational device of "myths" to examine conventional educational assumptions, adopting a demythologising pedagogical posture. However, a qualitative sociology textbook does not become methodologically inconsistent merely because it relies upon discourse analysis rather than controlled laboratory experiments. The methodological question is not whether qualitative, historical, or philosophical reasoning is legitimate, but whether comparable empirical and conceptual claims are subjected to appropriately comparable standards of justification.

This potential asymmetry becomes particularly important where historical interpretations are presented as explanatory foundations for broader claims about education and development. For instance, the discussion of pre-adulthood draws upon Philippe Ariès's (1962) influential thesis concerning the historical construction of childhood—a thesis that has subsequently been substantially qualified by subsequent medievalists. The problem is not Tait et al.'s use of Ariès itself, nor the legitimacy of historical interpretation, but the evidentiary status accorded to the resulting claim. As Orme (2001, pp. 4–5, 9) demonstrates, medieval society recognised childhood as a distinct developmental stage, with specialised clothing, forms of play, and legal distinctions including doli incapax. The historical evidence therefore complicates any strong formulation in which childhood is presented primarily as a modern institutional invention. Furthermore, when the development of mass schooling is interpreted primarily through its relationship with industrialisation and social regulation, such an account requires careful differentiation between the economic functions of schooling, the administrative requirements generated by mass education, and stronger claims regarding institutional design. Equating qualitative textual interpretation with unmediated truth while treating quantitative metrics with generalized skepticism creates a persistent methodological tension.

III. Analogical Extension, Carceral Surveillance, and the Psy-Complex

The extension of Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power, developed substantially through his examination of penal institutions, to everyday school administration represents another area where sociological theory risks overextending its explanatory reach. Foucault's analysis provides a useful vocabulary for examining how timetables, record-keeping, surveillance, and examination can contribute to the regulation of conduct. The methodological difficulty arises when the identification of disciplinary mechanisms is allowed to substitute for an explanation of the institutional purposes those mechanisms serve. School attendance, spatial organisation, and supervision may indeed produce forms of visibility and normalisation; however, their educational, administrative, and child-protection functions cannot be inferred from their disciplinary characteristics alone.

Mass education necessarily involves substantial spatial, temporal, and administrative coordination, and these functions cannot be adequately understood solely in terms of surveillance. An analysis that foregrounds surveillance without adequately accounting for these functions risks presenting legitimate forms of professional coordination primarily as mechanisms of social control. Similarly, in examining subjectivity and the psy-complex, the text frames conditions like Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) primarily through the lens of institutional social control (Rose, 1999). A sociological analysis of diagnostic categorisation can illuminate how diagnostic categories acquire institutional functions; however, such an analysis does not by itself constitute an adequate account of the condition. A comprehensive explanation also requires engagement with contemporary evidence concerning neurodevelopment, cognition, genetics, and environmental interaction, risking the marginalization of genuine neurobiological variation if treated as exclusively socio-political.

IV. Caricatures of Meritocracy, Datafication, and Deductive Overreach

Underpinning these theoretical positions are recurring argumentative problems regarding institutional incentives and measurement. In examining social class and equity (Chapter 1), the text critiques meritocracy by focusing on how systemic background conditions skew outcomes. However, the critique becomes vulnerable when it treats structural conditioning as an absolute negation of individual effort, prior knowledge, motivation, and cognitive development. Recognition of structural inequality is not incompatible with recognising individual agency, prior knowledge, motivation, and cognitive development as additional determinants of educational outcomes.

Similarly, when addressing standardized testing and datafication (Chapter 9), the treatment of standardised assessment is predominantly framed through its relationship to datafication, governance, and surveillance. An exclusively critical treatment of standardized assessment risks underweighting legitimate functions of assessment, including monitoring student attainment, identifying disparities, and informing resource allocation. Furthermore, the framework occasionally commits deductive overreaches—such as assuming that because macro-systemic policy operates under neoliberal logic (Chapter 7), every local pedagogical decision or teacher-led lesson plan is inherently a mechanism of overarching control. A macro-level analysis of neoliberal policy can, however, leave insufficient analytical space for variation in how teachers interpret, negotiate, adapt, and resist policy requirements in local practice, culminating in Chapter 8 where the explanatory emphasis on social construction risks underweighting biological maturation milestones.

V. Bourdieusian Theory and the Risk of Structural Determinism

Pierre Bourdieu’s (1986) framework of cultural capital, *habitus*, and symbolic violence offers vital tools for understanding how social privilege is maintained. However, difficulties arise when interpretations treat *habitus* as overwhelmingly determinative rather than as a structured disposition that interacts with shifting fields. Treating *habitus* as effectively predictive of individual conduct risks converting a theory of structured dispositions into a deterministic model of behaviour, minimizing human agency, student resilience, and the capacity of explicit instruction to improve domain learning across diverse socioeconomic backgrounds.

This theoretical tension has direct implications for classroom practice. When academic English and formal disciplinary knowledge are interpreted predominantly as mechanisms through which existing power relations are reproduced, well-meaning educators may receive insufficient guidance concerning the pedagogical importance of explicitly teaching the linguistic and disciplinary conventions required for participation in academic and professional institutions. As Lisa Delpit (1995) demonstrated in her seminal pedagogical critiques, recognizing the historical contingency of linguistic authority does not entail withholding access to the forms of language that carry institutional power. Where access to dominant linguistic and disciplinary registers is unequally distributed through families and communities, the school may be one of the principal institutions through which students without that prior exposure acquire them. Denying students explicit access to high-status disciplinary knowledge under the guise of critiquing power structures risks compounding educational exclusion.

This is further illuminated by Basil Bernstein's (1971) sociolinguistic code theory. While Bernstein highlighted the distinction between restricted and elaborated codes, educational theory must take care not to pathologize non-standard language. As William Labov (1972) demonstrated through his empirical work on Black English Vernacular, non-standard dialects possess complex internal grammatical structures. Sociological critique must frame academic language not solely as an instrument of class oppression, but as a public, learnable register that provides access to the forms of academic, professional, and institutional communication through which knowledge is conventionally taught, assessed, and exchanged.

VI. Poststructuralist Tensions in Gender, Identity, and Curricular Relativism

The analytical tensions of critical sociology are laid particularly bare in its treatment of gender, subjectivity, and sexuality through poststructuralist frameworks (Foucault, 1977; Butler, 1990). Within governmentality and performativity frameworks, individual agency is conceptualised within accounts of subject formation in which persons are strongly constituted by social and discursive relations. If discursive constitution is treated as a sufficiently comprehensive explanation of action, however, the framework risks under-specifying agency and intentionality.

Moreover, the text navigates an analytical tension regarding identity categories. It adopts a deconstructive stance toward identity, teaching that categories such as woman, man, and lesbian are historically contingent and regulatory categories produced by power matrices. Yet, when addressing discrimination or systemic inequality, the framework treats these exact identity categories as relatively stable political and administrative categories. Rather than an absolute logical contradiction, this represents an operational tension: using a category politically does not require believing that category possesses an immutable metaphysical essence. The difficulty therefore arises not from the political use of identity categories itself, but from failing to distinguish between their practical stability for purposes of policy and their theoretical contingency as objects of analysis. Furthermore, framing gender and sexuality primarily through semiotic regimes risks sidelining material inequalities such as pay disparities and workplace discrimination unless accompanied by structural economic analysis.

Finally, postcolonial and constructivist critiques (Fanon, 1967; Spivak, 1988) must distinguish between acknowledging the historical colonial context of knowledge production and committing a genetic fallacy that dismisses specialized knowledge as invalid. Acknowledging the colonial history of an academic discipline does not, by itself, establish that the knowledge produced within that discipline is epistemically invalid. The pedagogical danger arises when historical critique is allowed to displace rather than contextualise disciplinary knowledge. As Michael Young (2008) argues through Social Realism, specialized disciplinary knowledge is not an arbitrary ruling-class power toy; rather, access to specialised disciplinary knowledge can provide learners with conceptual resources that extend beyond their immediate experiences.

VII. Pedagogical Implications of an Incomplete Explanatory Framework

When unexamined sociological overreaches cascade from academic texts into pre-service teacher training curricula, state policy frameworks, and classroom practice, they generate tangible conceptual risks for educational equity. The pedagogical concern is not that exposure to critical sociology necessarily produces negative outcomes, but that when a critical framework is presented without sufficiently developed countervailing accounts of cognition, development, disciplinary knowledge, and assessment, pre-service teachers may receive an incomplete account of the factors that inform effective practice. The issue is therefore one of pedagogical completeness rather than a claim that critical sociology directly causes particular classroom behaviours. Most notably, when explicit instruction and traditional academic codes are framed primarily through the lens of symbolic violence, pre-service teachers may hesitate to directly impart the linguistic and cognitive tools disadvantaged students require for academic mobility. Similarly, when basic organizational routines and discipline are interpreted exclusively through a framework of surveillance and control, it can contribute to teacher frustration and undermine the institutional confidence necessary to maintain orderly, safe learning environments.

Furthermore, if a strongly social-constructionist account of neurodevelopmental conditions is treated as explanatorily sufficient, it risks undermining recognition of genuine executive function challenges, potentially leaving the resulting framework with insufficient guidance concerning the recognition of neurodevelopmental differences and the accommodations they may require. Conversely, an exclusively critical treatment of standardized assessment and quantitative metrics can leave policymakers less able to identify resource inequities or audit funding distribution. A critical examination of the historical and cultural conditions under which scientific and disciplinary knowledge is produced does not, without further argument, establish that such knowledge is epistemically invalid or pedagogically dispensable. For teacher education, the distinction matters because critique of the social conditions of knowledge production and access to disciplinary knowledge are not mutually exclusive projects. Finally, a deterministic interpretation of structural disadvantage could create a pedagogical risk: if educational outcomes are presented as largely predetermined by social position, teachers may have less conceptual space for understanding instruction as an intervention capable of changing those outcomes.

VIII. Conclusion: Reclaiming Critical Theory as an Auxiliary Tool

The critical sociology of education provides a valuable diagnostic lens for identifying systemic inequalities, but it is insufficient as a standalone framework for comprehensive pedagogical practice. Its insights regarding cultural capital, audit cultures, and structural bias remain vital for educator reflexivity (Ball, 2003; Lingard, 2010). However, difficulties arise when critical theory privileges particular forms of sociological interpretation over competing empirical explanations, discounting developmental biology, cognitive science, and assessment utility. Professional educator preparation requires a plural explanatory framework: structural analysis of inequality alongside evidence concerning development, cognition, language, assessment, subject knowledge, and pedagogy. Critical sociology should be retained as a diagnostic resource rather than treated as a comprehensive theory of educational causation or a complete basis for teacher preparation.
